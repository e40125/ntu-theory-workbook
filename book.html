The whole idea

This deck gives us the grammar for talking precisely about computers. Before proving anything, ask: what objects are involved, and what exactly am I claiming about them? A bunch of notation becomes much less cursed once every symbol has a job. Pages 1–9 introduce objects and logic; pages 10–15 show how definitions become reasons in a proof.

Aight, first stop: what kind of thing are we even holding? A shopping bag, a queue and a filled-out form are all collections, but you cannot swap their rules around. Sets, sequences and tuples have the same issue. If we identify the object first, the symbols stop looking like random-ass punctuation.

Round 1 — Sort the damn objects before manipulating them · pages 1–6

Imagine a contact list, a queue and a form. A set behaves like a contact list where the question is “is this person included?” Adding the same person twice does not create a new member. A sequence behaves like a queue: who comes first matters, and repeated entries remain. A tuple is an ordered record with a fixed number of fields. These are analogies for membership and order, not claims about software implementation.

Now read the symbols as complete sentences. x∈A means x is a member of A. x∉A means it is not. A⊆B means every member of A is also in B; A may equal B. The slides sometimes use ⊂ for subset, so check the intended convention rather than assuming “strictly smaller.” ∅ is a set with no members. {∅} has one member: the empty set. An empty box and a box containing an empty box are different objects.

A Cartesian product A×B makes ordered pairs, one choice from each set. The order fixes the fields: (1,x) means number first, letter second. A function f:A→B promises one output in B for each input in A. Its domain is A; B is the codomain of allowed outputs. It need not use every output, and two inputs may share an output. A relation merely records which combinations satisfy a condition. The scissors-paper-stone table on page 2 is a relation: T means “the row choice beats the column choice.”

For page 3, ∀x means “for every x”; ⇒ means “implies”; ⇔ means “in both directions.” The three equivalence conditions prevent a grouping rule from behaving inconsistently. Modulo 7 means remainder after division by 7. Thus 2, 9 and 16 belong to one remainder class even though they are different integers.

A graph consists of vertices and edges, not merely circles on paper. Undirected edges join unordered endpoints; directed edges have a source and destination. A subgraph chooses some vertices and some original edges between chosen vertices. It cannot invent a connection absent from the original or retain an edge whose endpoint was removed. An induced subgraph, a stronger condition, must retain every original edge between its chosen vertices; ordinary subgraphs need not.

Round 2 — Why “if” is such a sneaky little word · pages 7–10

AND, written ∧, requires both statements. OR, written ∨, allows either or both. NOT, written ¬, reverses truth. XOR permits exactly one. In Boolean calculations 0 and 1 mean false and true; elsewhere they may be literal input symbols. Same marks, different jobs.

“If P, then Q” makes a promise about cases where P holds. It is broken only by P=true and Q=false. That explains all four rows of the implication table: false/false and false/true do not break the promise; true/true keeps it; true/false breaks it. This is why P→Q agrees with ¬P∨Q. It does not grant the reverse Q→P. Wet ground could come from a sprinkler.

A proof starts with what is granted and earns what is claimed. Direct proof: assume P, derive Q. Contrapositive: assume ¬Q, derive ¬P. Contradiction: assume the claim fails, derive something impossible. The page 10 label is loose; its displayed ¬Q→¬P is specifically the contrapositive. Do not memorize the loose label and carry that confusion into an exam.

Round 3 — Every missing step in the square-root argument · pages 11–15

A rational number can be written m/n with integers m,n and n≠0. “Lowest terms” means cancel all common factors first. An irrational number has no such representation. Assume √2=m/n in lowest terms. Squaring gives 2=m²/n², so 2n²=m². Therefore m² is even.

Why does that force m even? Every odd integer has the form 2k+1. Its square is 4k²+4k+1, which is odd. So an even square cannot come from an odd m. Write m=2k, substitute, and obtain 2n²=4k², hence n²=2k². The same reasoning makes n even. Now m and n both contain factor 2, contradicting our lowest-terms choice. The assumption that √2 was rational must fail.

The even-degree argument uses a different engine: count the same quantity from the edges instead of from the vertices. The square-root argument uses a forbidden consequence of the assumption. “Proof” is not one magic sentence pattern; it is a chain in which each step has a reason.

Build the objects from scratch

A set is a collection where membership matters. {0,1} and {1,0,1} name the same set. A sequence preserves order and repetitions: 01 differs from 10, and 11 differs from 1. A tuple is a fixed-length sequence of entries. A machine's five-tuple is therefore a record with five different fields, not five arbitrary decorations.

A function gives exactly one output for every allowed input. Its output may itself be a set. That is how an NFA transition can be a perfectly valid function even though the machine has several possible next states. A relation records which pairs satisfy a condition; it need not select a single partner.

The Cartesian product lists ordered choices from two sets. If A={1,2} and B={x,y}, then A×B contains (1,x), (1,y), (2,x), (2,y). Later, “state × symbol” simply means “give me those two pieces of information together.”

Watch a definition do useful work

Pages 3–4 use “same remainder modulo 7.” Two integers are related when their difference is a multiple of 7. Why is this an equivalence relation—a way of grouping genuinely interchangeable cases?

An integer differs from itself by zero, so it relates to itself (reflexive). If i−j=7a, then j−i=7(−a), so reversing the pair preserves the relation (symmetric). If i−j=7a and j−k=7b, adding gives i−k=7(a+b), so the relationship passes through the middle number (transitive). Each fancy word has just become a checkable property.

How a proof grows out of the picture

Page 11 asks why an undirected graph's total degree is even. A vertex is a node; an edge joins endpoints; a vertex's degree counts incident edge-ends. Look at one edge. It contributes one end at each endpoint, so two contributions overall. Repeat that accounting for every edge: total degree = twice the number of edges. Twice an integer is even. Done. We counted a generic contribution, so the argument covers every finite graph, not just the drawn example. A self-loop, if allowed, contributes two to its vertex's degree.

An implication “P implies Q” says there cannot be a case with P true and Q false. It does not say Q happens only because of P. “Rain implies wet ground” allows wet ground from a sprinkler. To disprove a universal implication, find one case satisfying P but violating Q.

Source wording to separate: page 10 writes the contrapositive ¬Q→¬P under a contradiction heading. Contraposition proves that equivalent implication. Contradiction assumes the original claim fails and derives an impossibility. Pages 13–15 use contradiction: write √2=m/n in lowest terms; its square forces m even, then n even, contradicting lowest terms. Those methods are related but their opening assumptions differ.

The whole idea

The design question is: which differences between input histories can safely be forgotten? If two histories require the same behavior for every possible continuation, one state may represent both. If some continuation must distinguish them, merging them loses information. This is the reason to design state meanings before drawing arrows.

Say I hide the input you already read. What facts would you need me to leave on a sticky note so you can finish the decision? That sticky note is the state. For “same first and last symbol,” forgetting the first symbol wrecks the job, even if you remember the last one perfectly.

Round 1 — The first letter stays; the last letter keeps changing · pages 1–4

For strings beginning and ending with the same symbol, one memory slot is not enough. You need to retain the first symbol and update the most recent one. The slide uses a separate start s, then two states for strings starting with a and two for strings starting with b. Only the matching-end state in each branch accepts. A one-letter a counts: its first and last symbol are the same occurrence. Empty input has no first symbol, so the start is nonaccepting.

Picture pinning the first character to a clipboard and overwriting a “latest character” field. The pinned field explains why you cannot merge the a-start and b-start branches merely because both currently end in a. A future a may accept one and reject the other.

The proof has two obligations. If the machine accepts, its recorded first and last symbols agree. If a nonempty string's first and last symbols agree, the updates put it in the corresponding accepting state. The first direction forbids false positives; the second forbids missed valid strings. “It worked on aba” establishes neither universal direction.

Round 2 — Modulo is a tiny scoreboard · pages 5–8

The reset example remembers the sum of the current segment modulo 3: only remainder 0, 1 or 2. Reading digit d changes remainder r to (r+d) mod 3. This sums digits; it is not interpreting the entire input as a base-three integer. Reading reset discards the old segment and sets remainder to 0.

Trace the slide's 10⟨reset⟩22⟨reset⟩012: remainders 0→1→1→0→2→1→0→0→1→0. End remainder zero means accept. The reset token is one alphabet symbol. Angle brackets here visually name that token; they are not extra characters to feed the machine.

Round 3 — The formal run is just a receipt for every step · pages 9–10

Write input w=w₁…wₙ. The subscript identifies a position: w₁ is the first symbol, wₙ the last; n is length. Write r₀…rₙ for the visited states. There are n+1 state entries because we record the starting situation before consuming any symbol.

r₀=q₀ checks the receipt starts correctly. δ(rᵢ,wᵢ₊₁)=rᵢ₊₁ checks each update uses the actual table. rₙ∈F checks the ending state. The symbol ∃ means “there exists.” In a DFA the valid run is unique, but the formal acceptance statement can still say such a run exists. It is a definition of accepting runs, not a command that every input must accept.

A language is regular if some finite automaton recognizes it exactly. “Some” means at least one, not exactly one. Showing your attempted machine fails does not prove no machine exists. Conversely, producing one correct finite machine is enough to establish regularity.

Round 4 — Remember a useful suffix, not the whole damn string · pages 11–14

For odd numbers of 1s, each 1 toggles even↔odd; 0 preserves the count's parity. Parity means evenness or oddness. Empty input has zero ones, so start even and reject it.

For “contains 001,” before finding the pattern retain the longest ending piece that could begin 001: nothing useful, 0, or 00. From 00 on 0, stay at 00, because the newest two zeros still could start a match. From 0 on 1, return to no useful suffix. From 00 on 1, the match is complete; stay accepting on every later symbol. Once a substring has occurred, later input cannot un-occur it. That is why this accepting state loops forever, whereas the ends-in-1 accepting state did not.

Build the picture from the slide examples

Pages 1–4 recognize nonempty strings whose first and last symbols agree. You need the first symbol permanently, and you update the current last symbol. A separate initial situation handles “nothing read yet.” One symbol such as a already has matching first and last symbols; the empty string needs its own decision.

Pages 6–8 keep the sum since the last reset, modulo 3. Modulo 3 means the remainder after division by 3: 0, 1 or 2. You can throw away the actual sum because adding the next digit affects the final remainder using only the current remainder. A reset overwrites the summary with remainder zero. This is deliberate forgetting, not random loss of memory.

One complete construction: odd many ones

Pages 11–13 ask for binary strings with an odd number of 1s. “Odd” means 1,3,5,…; “even” means 0,2,4,…. You do not need to distinguish one 1 from a thousand and one. Both require the same future toggles.

Let qₑ mean “even many ones so far,” and qₒ mean “odd many.” Start qₑ because the initial count is zero. Accept at qₒ. Reading 0 changes no count, so each state loops on 0. Reading 1 changes parity, so it switches states.

For 010101, the summaries after each symbol are even, odd, odd, even, even, odd. Accept. If you append another 1, the answer flips. That is exactly what the rule demands.

The formal computation page is a run log

On page 9, w=w₁…wₙ names the input symbols. The r₀,…,rₙ names the visited states. w entries are what we read; r entries are what we remember afterward. There are n symbols but n+1 recorded states because r₀ records the situation before reading anything.

r₀=q₀ says start correctly. δ(rᵢ,wᵢ₊₁)=rᵢ₊₁ says each adjacent row follows the update rule. rₙ∈F says the final recorded state belongs to the accepting set. “∈” means membership. It is an acceptance test, not a claim that every run must accept.

A language is regular if some finite automaton recognizes exactly its strings. That is an existence claim about a checker. A language does not become regular because its English description sounds simple.

Why this method generalizes

The odd/even invariant holds at the start and survives each symbol. That gives a proof for arbitrarily long inputs. The “contains 001” machine on page 14 uses another summary: how much of the useful suffix 001 has been matched, or whether it was already found. After 000, the useful suffix is still 00; an extra zero should not erase both zeros from memory.

The reset, parity and substring examples share one mental move: keep exactly the information needed for later decisions. Their arrows differ because their meanings differ.

A proof starts with what is granted and earns what is claimed. Direct proof: assume P, derive Q. Contrapositive: assume ¬Q, derive ¬P. Contradiction: assume the claim fails, derive something impossible. The page 10 label is loose; its displayed ¬Q→¬P is specifically the contrapositive. Round 3 — Every missing step in the square-root argument · pages 11–15

A rational number can be written m/n with integers m,n and n≠0. “Lowest terms” means cancel all common factors first. An irrational number has no such representation. Assume √2=m/n in lowest terms. Squaring gives 2=m²/n², so 2n²=m². Therefore m² is even.

Why does that force m even? Every odd integer has the form 2k+1. Its square is 4k²+4k+1, which is odd. So an even square cannot come from an odd m. Write m=2k, substitute, and obtain 2n²=4k², hence n²=2k². The same reasoning makes n even. Now m and n both contain factor 2, contradicting our lowest-terms choice. The assumption that √2 was rational must fail.

The even-degree argument uses a different engine: count the same quantity from the edges instead of from the vertices. The square-root argument uses a forbidden consequence of the assumption. “Proof” is not one magic sentence pattern; it is a chain in which each step has a reason.

Start with the target, not fancy wording

Suppose the claim is: “If a DFA reaches the same state after strings u and v, then appending the same suffix z produces the same acceptance decision.”

What are you given? A deterministic machine, two runs ending at the same state, and one common remaining input z. What must you show? The final acceptance decisions agree. We don't need to remember the earlier strings; the current state plus the next symbol determines the next move.

Complete proof: Let q be the state reached after both u and v. The two remaining computations start at q and read the same string z. At each step, their current states and next input symbols are equal, so determinism gives equal next states. Hence their final states are equal. Since acceptance depends on whether that final state is in F, the decisions agree.

This is the engine behind the pumping lemma. Once you're back in the same state, the machine has no secret counter remembering how many laps you took. The state is the stored summary; the circle is how we draw it. There is no extra hidden memory in the model.

Make the repeated-step reasoning explicit: induction

Induction proves a claim for every nonnegative integer by covering a starting case and a repeatable step. Think of a rule that lets you extend a correct run by one more symbol.

Claim: after k symbols of the common suffix above, the two machines are in the same state.

  • Base, k=0: neither suffix symbol has been read. Both states are q by assumption.
  • Induction hypothesis: assume the states agree after k symbols.
  • Step: if another symbol exists, both next symbols are the same symbol a. Apply δ to the same pair (state,a); the resulting states agree after k+1 symbols.
  • Conclusion: the states agree after all |z| symbols, including when |z|=0.

The hypothesis is a temporary assumption used to prove the next case. It is not permission to assume the final claim with no work. This pattern also proves a machine invariant: describe exactly what each state or stack represents after any processed prefix, verify the start, then verify every possible update.

Equality needs two directions

To prove a grammar generates exactly L, show:

  1. Soundness: every string the grammar generates is in L. No extra garbage gets through.
  2. Completeness: every string in L has a derivation. No valid input is excluded.

Worked example: S→0S1∣εS\to0S1\mid\varepsilonS→0S1∣ε generates exactly {0n1n∣n≥0}\{0^n1^n\mid n\geq0\}{0n1n∣n≥0}.

Soundness: A terminating derivation uses the wrapping rule some k≥0 times and then S→ε. Each wrap adds one 0 on the left and one 1 on the right, so the result is 0ᵏ1ᵏ.

Completeness: Take any 0ⁿ1ⁿ with n≥0. Apply S→0S1 exactly n times, then S→ε. This derives the chosen string. The two inclusions prove equality.

Contradiction and counterexamples

To prove L is not regular, assume it is regular and show this forces a statement incompatible with that assumption. You must name the contradiction. “This is impossible” is not a reason by itself.

To disprove “every binary string with equal counts is zeros-then-ones,” one counterexample suffices: 0101 has equal counts but is not in block order. You do not need to show that all equal-count strings fail. Which side says “every” matters.

∀\forall∀ means “for every”; ∃\exists∃ means “there exists.” Negating “every input is accepted” gives “some input is not accepted.” Negating “some input is accepted” gives “every input is not accepted.” Swap the quantifier and negate its claim. The question: when is a finite number of states not enough?

A DFA can remember parity with two states. Could it remember the exact number of leading zeros so it can demand that many ones afterward? For {0n1n:n≥0}\{0^n1^n:n\ge0\}{0n1n:n≥0}, the number may be arbitrarily large. “It needs to count” is a useful suspicion, but not yet a proof: plenty of counting tasks only need a remainder. We need a consequence that every DFA must obey.

Suppose a DFA has p states. Read p symbols. The computation has visited p+1 state positions: the initial state plus one after each symbol. With only p different states, some state was visited twice. That's the pigeonhole principle: more visits than available distinct places forces a repeat.

A finite-state checker eventually has to reuse a memory situation on a long enough accepted input. Reusing a state creates a loop. Now we can replay that loop and ask whether the language’s rule survives. That is the whole engine; the theorem’s alphabet soup tells us exactly when we are allowed to use it.

Round 1 — “It needs to count” is a suspicion, not a proof · pages 1–2

For 0ⁿ1ⁿ, the machine must match an arbitrarily large number of leading zeros with trailing ones. Finite memory sounds inadequate, but the exam needs an argument ruling out every possible DFA design. Saying “I can't draw one” only reports your own failed design attempt.

The adjacent-pattern example shows why intuition can screw you over. C requires equally many individual 0s and 1s. D requires equally many adjacent 01 and 10 occurrences. An occurrence is an adjacent pair; overlaps count. In 01010, there are two 01s and two 10s, but three zeros and two ones.

Every 01 is a switch into the 1-region, and every 10 switches back. For a nonempty binary string, the two counts balance exactly when first and last symbols agree. If it starts 0 and ends 1, there is one extra 01; reverse endpoints give one extra 10. ε has both counts zero too. So D is regular: remember the first and current last symbol, with ε accepted. Individual counts C do not collapse into that bounded endpoint summary.

Round 2 — Who chooses what in the theorem? · pages 3–4

The statement is: if A is regular, there is an integer p≥1 such that every accepted string s with length at least p has some split s=xyz satisfying three requirements. y is nonempty; x and y together fit within the first p symbols; repeating y any number i≥0 keeps the string in A.

p is a threshold, not a chosen example string. x is the prefix before the loop, y the loop's consumed substring, z the suffix after it. Concatenation xyz means those exact three pieces in that order. y⁰=ε deletes y; y¹=y leaves it once; y²=yy doubles it. x and z stay fixed.

The quantifier order matters: ∃p, then ∀s, then ∃x,y,z, then ∀i. “For every” means all allowed choices; “there exists” means at least one suitable choice. The split can depend on s. Once selected, it must work for every i. A different successful split for every i is not what the theorem says.

This is a necessary condition for regularity. Regularity guarantees it. Showing a few strings pump does not establish regularity. To use the lemma against regularity, we must show the promised condition cannot hold.

Round 3 — The loop is forced by too many visits and too few rooms · pages 5–9

Assume a DFA for A has p states. Reading the first p symbols records p+1 visits, including the starting state before any input. Pigeonhole principle: placing more visits than available states forces two visits to the same state. Choose their positions j<l in the slide's q₁,…,qₙ₊₁ numbering.

The characters before the first visit form x=s₁…sⱼ₋₁. Those between the visits form y=sⱼ…sₗ₋₁. The rest form z=sₗ…sₙ. The two visits are distinct positions, so l−j>0 and y is nonempty. Both are among the first p+1 visits, so l−1≤p and |xy|≤p.

At the end of x the machine is at the loop state. Reading y returns to that same state. Reading it twice returns there twice; skipping it leaves the machine there already. In every case the unchanged z starts from the same state and reaches the old accepting endpoint. That's the mechanism behind “for all i.”

If A has no strings as long as p, the conditional promise about long members has no counterexample. This is called vacuous truth. The lemma does not insist every regular language contain long strings. A finite language is regular and may satisfy the threshold condition simply because there are no eligible strings.

Why a loop is unavoidable

Suppose a DFA has p states. Reading its first p symbols visits p+1 states when you include the start. There are more visits than available states, so two visits must be to the same state. This is the pigeonhole principle: p boxes cannot hold p+1 objects with at most one in each.

Split an accepted input into x, y, z. x gets to the repeated state; y goes around the loop and returns; z is the rest of the input leading to acceptance. The loop consumes at least one symbol because the two visits occur at different times. It finishes within the first p input symbols.

Take a concrete conditional example: 00 [0] 111. Suppose the bracketed 0 loops back to the same state. Doing that loop twice gives 00 [00] 111; doing it zero times gives 00 [] 111, or 00111. All three computations reach the same state before the final 111, so the same suffix leads to acceptance. Yet only 000111 has equal numbers of zeros and ones. This explains the mechanism. It does not claim every DFA has its loop at this exact position.

  • p: the pumping length; the hypothetical machine supplies it.

Pumping proofs have a fixed negotiation. Round 1 — Negation reverses who has to supply the evidence · pages 1–7

“For every n, a formula holds” is disproved by finding one n where it fails. “There exists a successful choice” is disproved by showing every allowed choice fails. That is why ∀ and ∃ swap when negated. The condition itself must be negated too.

Start with what a grammar is trying to do

A DFA checks a completed string. A grammar builds strings. We still care about a language—a set of completed strings—but now ask which ones the construction rules can produce. There is no input head and no accepting circle in a grammar.

The deck's grammar is:

A→0A1∣B,B→#.A\to0A1\mid B,\qquad B\to\#.A→0A1∣B,B→#.

A and B are variables: placeholders for unfinished work. 0, 1, and # are terminals: finished output symbols that stay put. A is the starting variable. The vertical bar means “or,” so A has two alternative rules. The arrow means “you may replace this variable with that right-hand side.”

Aight, we are changing jobs: checking a finished string versus building possible strings. A variable is an unfinished job ticket. A rule replaces that ticket with new work or finished output. Terminals are the printed symbols that stay. Follow one ticket until the entire line contains finished output; that is a derivation.

Round 1 — A grammar builds; a recognizer checks · pages 1–5

Aight, switch jobs deliberately. A machine starts with an input and checks it. A grammar starts with a pending instruction and builds possible outputs. Its language is the collection of completed outputs. These are two ways to describe which strings belong, not two names for the same physical process.

In the slide grammar A→0A1, A→B, B→#, A and B are variables: unfinished jobs. The symbols 0,1,# are terminals: finished output that no grammar rule rewrites. Here # is a literal printed symbol, not “number of.” A is the start variable.

Round 4 — Balanced parentheses require order as well as counts · page 12

The question: who belongs together?

The characters in a+a×a do not visibly say which operation groups first. A grammar can encode that structure. In the deck's layered grammar,

E→E+T∣T,T→T×F∣F,F→(E)∣a,E\to E+T\mid T,\quad T\to T\times F\mid F,\quad F\to(E)\mid a,E→E+T∣T,T→T×F∣F,F→(E)∣a,

E means an expression built from terms, T a multiplication-level term, and F an atomic factor: a single a or a parenthesized expression. These names are helpful meanings assigned to variables; the rules do the enforcing.

The expression can have one spelling and two different structures underneath. Think of “add first” versus “multiply first”: same visible ingredients, different grouping. The grammar has to tell us which groups belong together. Merely changing the order in which you expand independent jobs does not create a new structure.

Round 1 — The grammar can enforce which operation binds first · pages 1–3

An expression may contain additions. A term may contain multiplications but no unparenthesized addition at its own level. A factor is either the terminal a or a parenthesized expression. The rules put those three jobs at different levels:

E→E+T∣T,T→T×F∣F,F→(E)∣a.E\to E+T\mid T,\quad T\to T\times F\mid F,\quad F\to(E)\mid a.E→E+T∣T,T→T×F∣F,F→(E)∣a.

These E,T,F abbreviate the slide's long variable labels. They are variables, while +,×,(,) and a are terminals. The grammar describes syntactic structure; a is a placeholder operand, not a variable we rewrite with arithmetic values.

For a+a×a, the top split is E+T. The left E finishes as a; the right T splits as T×F and finishes as a×a. Thus the multiplication forms one subtree inside the addition. For (a+a)×a, the top split is T×F; the left factor uses (E), allowing the addition inside parentheses. Parentheses explicitly create a factor containing an entire expression. That's how the hierarchy encodes precedence.

To generate 0ⁿ1ⁿ or 1ⁿ0ⁿ, use separate variables S₁ and S₂ for the two languages, then S→S₁|S₂. Choose one whole branch, not arbitrary alternating rules from both jobs. Rename variables before combining grammars if their old names collide.

A DFA can also become a grammar. Create variable Rᵢ for state qᵢ. For transition δ(qᵢ,a)=qⱼ add Rᵢ→aRⱼ. For accepting qᵢ add Rᵢ→ε. Start at the variable corresponding to the DFA start. A derivation prints precisely the labels of a path and may finish precisely at an accepting state.

Such a rule is right-linear: at most one variable remains, at the right end. General CFG rules can leave work on both sides, such as Rᵢ→aRⱼb. That pending b is an obligation the DFA's simple path rule cannot directly express. “A regular CFG” informally means a CFG whose generated language happens to be regular; regularity is a property of the language, not a guarantee from the letters used.

Round 3 — Different schedules are not necessarily different structures · pages 7–12

The loose grammar E→E+E|E×E|(E)|a permits two trees for a+a×a. One makes + the root, representing a+(a×a); the other makes × the root, representing (a+a)×a. Those parenthesized descriptions explain the trees; they are not extra terminals inserted into the original string.

To prove ambiguity using derivations, force a common schedule: always expand the leftmost remaining variable. Two distinct leftmost derivations of one terminal string establish two different trees. For the loose grammar:

E ⇒ E+E ⇒ a+E ⇒ a+E×E ⇒ a+a×E ⇒ a+a×a
E ⇒ E×E ⇒ E+E×E ⇒ a+E×E ⇒ a+a×E ⇒ a+a×a

The second line's first E is expanded before the later E. Both are leftmost; their top-level rules differ. By contrast, expanding the right child before the left child of one fixed tree changes the work schedule, not its parse structure. That's why “two arbitrary derivations” is too weak a definition.

A grammar is ambiguous when at least one string has two parse trees. A language is inherently ambiguous when every CFG generating it is ambiguous. The slide's final wording loosely says “a CFG is inherently ambiguous”; the property being described belongs to the language. One bad grammar does not prove inherent ambiguity, because a different grammar may remove the ambiguity, as the E/T/F hierarchy does here.

Nullable rules can hide ambiguity

If B→BB|b|ε, the variable B can disappear directly or split and have both children disappear. So even a very short terminal string may have multiple parse structures. In the 2024 paper, S→B|BJ and J→jJb|j|ε. The string j has:

S⇒BJ⇒J⇒jS\Rightarrow BJ\Rightarrow J\Rightarrow jS⇒BJ⇒J⇒j

and

S⇒BJ⇒BBJ⇒BJ⇒J⇒j.S\Rightarrow BJ\Rightarrow BBJ\Rightarrow BJ\Rightarrow J\Rightarrow j.S⇒BJ⇒BBJ⇒BJ⇒J⇒j.

The second route has an extra B→BB subtree. Deleting ε from a written string does not delete that historical tree structure. This is why identical-looking intermediate strings can occur in distinct derivations.

Grammar versus language

“G is ambiguous” is a statement about this grammar. It does not prove its language has no unambiguous grammar. A language is inherently ambiguous when every CFG for it is ambiguous. That is a stronger claim requiring a different argument. Don't upgrade the conclusion just because the wording sounds similar.

Identify a string that can be split in two ways, or produced by two alternative start branches, or gets redundant nullable subtrees. Write two complete leftmost derivations. The mental model: change the wiring, keep the possible outputs

Every conversion stage should answer the same question: “How can I reconstruct an old derivation from this new one, and a new derivation from an old one?” If you only check that the final grammar looks like CNF, you can still have the wrong language. Format and equivalence are separate obligations.

We need an order because one repair can create the next kind of unwanted rule. Removing ε rules can create unit rules, so doing unit removal first would leave new handoffs behind.

A recipe can delegate to another recipe that delegates straight back. If your cleanup keeps chasing that loop, you never finish. So we collect what is reachable, remember what we already found, and stop when nothing new appears. That stopping point is a fixed point; it is bookkeeping with a termination reason.

Round 1 — Stop playing whack-a-mole with ε rules · pages 1–2, 6–8

The source cycle S→B|ε, B→S|ε shows why naive editing can loop forever. Remove S→ε and B can recreate it; remove B→ε and S can recreate it. The grammar is finite, but an undisciplined procedure can keep repeating the same work.

Compute the nullable set first. Initially mark variables with an explicit ε rule. Repeatedly mark X when it has a rule whose entire right side consists of already-marked variables. A terminal blocks that reasoning because it cannot disappear. Stop when a complete pass adds no new variable. This stable result is a fixed point. Each productive pass marks something new and there are finitely many variables, so termination is guaranteed.

For the cycle, S and B are both nullable immediately. Add fresh S₀→S and preserve S₀→ε. Generate omission alternatives once, retaining originals, then remove ε rules of nonstart variables. Record handled combinations instead of reintroducing them indefinitely. The original language is only {ε}, so after eliminating unit-only dead ends the final useful CNF grammar can simply be S₀→ε.

Round 2 — A set of reachable delegates kills unit cycles · pages 3–4

A unit rule has exactly one variable on the right, such as A→B. For each X, compute Unit(X), the variables reachable from X using zero or more unit rules. Include X itself: its existing nonunit productions must survive. If X→Y and Y→Z, then Z belongs to Unit(X). If Z→X, you have a cycle, not an instruction to repeat forever.

For every Y in Unit(X), copy each nonunit Y→u as X→u. Then discard unit rules. Page 3's wording says to remove both A→B and B→u; deleting B→u globally would be unsafe when other rules still need B. The intended language-preserving move copies B's work to A and retains B's work. This is a source wording correction, not permission to silently alter the target grammar.

Round 3 — Make one complicated rule obey the two permitted shapes · page 5

Teaching example: X→aBCd. Introduce U→a and V→d. Replace the rule by X→UBCV, then split it into X→UY₁, Y₁→BY₂, Y₂→CV, using fresh helpers. Expand the helpers and you recover the exact ordered string aBCd.

Do not replace a rule X→a by X→U unnecessarily; that creates a forbidden unit rule. Terminal wrappers are needed when a terminal appears in a right side of length at least two. A right side of three or more variables needs a binary chain. A two-variable right side already has the correct shape.

Round 4 — Turn the recipe into checks you can actually use

After fresh start, nullable handling, unit handling, terminal isolation and binary splitting, inspect the result against the definition. Every ordinary alternative must be one terminal or two variables. The designated start must not occur on a right side. Its ε rule must be present exactly when the old language included ε.

Also inspect freshness: helper labels cannot accidentally collide with old variables. Inspect order: BC differs from CB. Inspect occurrences: three nullable appearances create up to 2³ choices before duplicates are merged. Duplicate productions do not change a grammar's language, but losing a distinct omission alternative can.

The conversion is systematic because each stage solves one precise violation. If a rule is still illegal at the end, identify which violation remains instead of restarting the whole procedure. The normal form is a tool for later algorithms; there is no claim that it makes the grammar smallest or easiest for a human to read.

Why the hell are we rewriting the grammar?

A rule can currently do several things at once: print terminals, create three pending jobs, hand off to one other variable, or produce nothing. That flexibility is convenient for writing a grammar, but awkward for algorithms that must reason about every derivation.

Chomsky normal form, CNF, limits a rule to one of two ordinary shapes:

A→BCorA→a.A\to BC\qquad\text{or}\qquad A\to a.A→BCorA→a.

The first splits one pending job into two pending jobs. The second finishes one job by printing one terminal. Capital letters here stand for variables, lowercase a for a terminal. They are shape examples, not the only letters allowed.

If ε belongs to the language, the new start may also have a rule to ε; that start must not appear on a right-hand side. CNF is a format for a grammar. It does not mean the language becomes regular, deterministic, or unambiguous.

Picture a job ticket. “Print a” completes it. “Do B, then C” tears it into two ordered subtickets. A variable is the ticket's label, not output printed on the page. This analogy maps only the pending-work structure: it does not introduce a real worker, time cost, or a rule that jobs must take equal time. CNF makes every ticket use one of those two shapes.

Aight, imagine a messy kitchen where a recipe step can do six jobs, delegate to another recipe, or produce nothing. CNF makes every ordinary instruction do one of two things: split into two jobs, or print one finished symbol. Same possible finished strings. Cleaner instructions. The hard part is preserving every choice while we clean this shit up.

Round 1 — Standardize the jobs without changing the outputs · pages 1–4

Think of converting a messy instruction manual into two permitted instruction types: “do these two subtasks, in order” or “print this one symbol.” That is the whole CNF project. We preserve the completed terminal strings. We do not promise to preserve every old intermediate string, tree shape or number of derivation steps.

A→BC has two variables on the right. A→a has one terminal. A→B is a pure handoff and forbidden; A→ab prints two terminals and is forbidden; A→aB mixes types and is forbidden. Only the start may have an ε rule, if empty input belongs to the language. The start must not appear on any right side. These are shape conditions, not claims about which letters you must use.

ASA means three consecutive jobs, in that order. ε is the empty string: no printed symbol. A production is one such replacement rule. The remaining S→A and A→S are pure handoffs. Going around S⇒A⇒S forever produces no finished word. What matters is which real work either variable can eventually choose. Why the transformations preserve the language

For ε elimination, each omitted occurrence represents a subtree that previously derived ε. A derivation in the new grammar can restore those omitted ε subtrees; an old derivation can remove them. For unit elimination, copied rules compress a finite chain of unit steps; expand the chain to recover the old derivation. Helpers merely group a long right side or name a terminal. Their expansion returns the original string in the original order.

These are explanations for arbitrary derivations. Testing short strings can catch a mistake, but it cannot prove grammar equality for all lengths.

Why give a machine a stack?

For 0n1n0^n1^n0n1n, after reading n zeros the machine owes n matching ones. A DFA has only finitely many states, so it cannot keep a different state for every possible debt. A PDA adds a stack: a potentially unbounded pile where we can touch only the top. Push one marker per zero; pop one per one. Now the size of the debt is stored outside the finite control.

The state remembers the phase—still reading zeros, or already matching ones. The stack remembers the amount owed. Mixing those jobs makes diagrams feel random. Separating them makes the arrows follow from the requirement.

Think of each pushed zero as an IOU: “a future 1 must pay for this.” The $ marker says there are no more IOUs underneath. The control state says whether issuing new IOUs is still allowed. Each zero hands the machine an IOU: “you owe me one matching one later.” The stack keeps the unpaid IOUs. The state remembers whether we are still collecting zeros or already paying with ones. Both jobs matter. A balanced total cannot rescue input whose zeros and ones arrived in the wrong order.

The bridge: generating a string and checking a string can cooperate

A grammar chooses how to build output. A PDA can guess those grammar choices while checking them against the supplied input. Its stack means: this is the sequence still owed by the current derivation. The next owed symbol belongs on top, so we can deal with it first.

The deck's grammar is S→aTb∣bS\to aTb\mid bS→aTb∣b, T→Ta∣εT\to Ta\mid\varepsilonT→Ta∣ε. S either produces a single b, or an a, then T's output, then b. T can produce any number of a's. So the language consists of b and all strings of one or more a's followed by one b.

The stack is the machine's to-do list, with the next job on top. Expanding a variable breaks a job into smaller jobs. Matching a terminal checks off finished output against the actual input. The stack changes jobs here. Instead of unpaid IOUs from earlier input, it stores the grammar’s future to-do list. The next job has to sit on top. That is why push order matters: shove the list on backward and the machine tries to print the sentence backward. Let’s make that ordering explicit.

For S→aTb, the next input to check is a, then whatever T generates, then b. The stack must therefore have a on top, T underneath, and b underneath T. Push b first, then T, then a. If you push a first, b ends up on top and the machine tries to read the output backward. That ain't a harmless implementation detail.

Initialize with $ and then S. At the main loop, if the top is a variable, nondeterministically choose one of its productions and replace it by the right side. If the top is a terminal t, consume exactly input t and pop it. If only $ remains, remove it and go to the accepting state.

A course PDA transition pushes at most one symbol, so pushing a whole right side is shorthand for a chain through fresh helper states. Those helper states must have only the intended continuation; otherwise another rule could interrupt halfway through the push. Rule expansion consumes ε input. Terminal matching consumes real input. If you include only expansion arrows, you can generate a stack forever while never checking a single input symbol.

Notice the subtlety: while T keeps expanding, those extra a's wait below it. They are not consumed until T is removed. Round 4 — Why guessing can't fabricate a wrong string · page 10

At main-loop checkpoints, the already-consumed input prefix followed by the pending stack string above $ is a sentential form derivable from S. A variable expansion applies a genuine production, preserving that statement. A terminal match shifts the first pending terminal into the consumed prefix without changing their concatenation.

If the machine accepts after consuming all input and exhausting pending work, the complete input was generated by the grammar. Conversely, choose a leftmost derivation of any grammar-generated string; the machine can choose the same productions and match its terminals, reaching acceptance. These are the two correctness directions.

A branch that reaches the accepting state with unread input does not accept that input. A branch that expands forever produces no finite accepting run. Neither invalidates the construction; the existence of a correct finite branch is what matters. Nonaccepting branches are not allowed to vote down the successful one.

What the sixteen variables are actually for

With four PDA states, the standard conversion introduces one variable Aₚᵩ for each ordered pair of states p,q: 4×4=16 variables. That does not mean the PDA suddenly has sixteen states. We are building a grammar whose variables summarize different kinds of journey through the original machine.

Aₚᵩ means: strings consumed by a run that starts in p with an empty stack and ends in q with an empty stack. The same run pattern can sit above a protected existing stack: it may build and clear extra work but never pop below that baseline. Merely having the same stack at both endpoints is not enough if the run disturbed the protected contents in between.

The PDA first needs a single accepting state, empty-stack completion, and transitions that each push or pop one symbol. The next deck explains that normalization carefully.

Think of a balanced segment as a subroutine that promises: “Whatever stack was underneath me, I'll leave it alone and clean up everything I add.” Aₚᵩ names that contract together with its entry and exit states. The grammar doesn't store a stack snapshot. It describes strings that can be read while honoring that contract.

Those subscripts name the start and finish of a whole trip. Imagine borrowing some workspace, doing a subroutine, then returning with the old workspace untouched. Aₚq names the input consumed by such a trip from p to q. It does not name one arrow. That distinction makes the 16 variables make sense.

Round 1 — Aₚq describes a whole balanced trip · pages 1–3

A variable Aₚq is one grammar variable whose label contains two state names. It describes input strings consumed by a computation that starts in p, ends in q, and leaves the underlying stack untouched. Picture a subroutine borrowing desk space above a sealed folder. It can stack temporary papers and remove them, but must leave the sealed folder intact. Round 4 — Why those three shapes cover every balanced computation

For soundness, induct on a finite derivation tree. Empty rules do nothing; concatenation joins two valid balanced trips; wrapping performs a matching push, a valid inner trip and its matching pop. Thus each generated string corresponds to a valid computation.

For completeness, induct on the number of transitions in a balanced computation. Zero transitions uses Aₚp→ε. If the stack returns to baseline at an intermediate point, split there and use a concatenation rule on the two shorter trips. Inducting on transitions, rather than input length, matters because ε moves can add transitions without adding characters.

One table cell isn't the whole decision

For a DFA, the current state and next input symbol select exactly one move. For a PDA, a move may read input or ε, and may pop the top or ε. Several differently labeled transitions can therefore be enabled at the same situation. Determinism must rule out those competing actions, not merely forbid two answers in one cell.

Suppose the machine is in q, the next input is 0, and the stack top is X. It could potentially use any of these:

δ(q,0,X) read the zero and pop X
δ(q,0,ε) read the zero without popping
δ(q,ε,X) pop X without reading
δ(q,ε,ε) move without reading or popping

If two are available, the machine has a choice right now. Different labels do not make that choice disappear.

Determinism must eliminate that competition too. Counting destinations inside separate table cells does not settle which actions are simultaneously available.

Round 1 — Determinism means one enabled action, not one arrow per label · pages 1–4

An NFA's nondeterminism can be compiled into a finite checklist because its possible states come from a fixed finite set. A PDA's configurations include arbitrarily long stacks. The analogous collection need not fit into finitely many states with one deterministic stack. The theorem is that deterministic context-free languages form a proper subfamily of context-free languages; this observation motivates it but is not the full separation proof.

The DPDA transition output is one pair (next state, pushed symbol) or ∅ for undefined. It no longer returns an arbitrary set of pairs. But that alone does not prevent conflicting moves, because ε moves can compete with symbol-consuming moves.

For current state q, upcoming actual symbol a and actual top x, four table entries could apply: δ(q,a,x), δ(q,a,ε), δ(q,ε,x), δ(q,ε,ε). They correspond to inspect both; read input without inspecting stack; inspect stack without reading input; inspect neither. If two were available, the machine would still have a choice even though each individual cell contained one output.

The course uses an exactly-one-defined condition for every q,a,x with a∈Σ and x∈Γ. Other presentations use an at-most-one condition with partial transitions. For the exam, use this course's stated convention. Exactly one is stronger: a situation with all four entries empty needs completion, usually through rejection. Do not swap conventions halfway through a construction.

Round 2 — ε in a pop slot does not ask whether the stack is empty

This deserves its own sentence because it causes so much trouble: ε in the pop slot means no stack symbol is required or removed. That move can apply when the stack is nonempty too. Therefore it competes with an x-pop move when x is actually on top. Similarly, an ε-input move may be available while real input remains.

The four-way condition quantifies actual x∈Γ. An empty stack has no actual top x. Only no-pop alternatives can work there; a defined pop transition cannot conjure a symbol out of emptiness. Determinism does not mean every input must successfully finish.

Round 3 — Two ways the unique run can fail · pages 5–6

First source example: the only defined action is an ε-input move that pops 0. With input 0 and empty stack, it cannot execute because the required 0 is absent. No input gets consumed, and the run is stuck. That rejects the input.

Second example: the only available action consumes ε, pops nothing and pushes 1. It can repeat forever, producing 1,11,111,… on the stack while the input 0 never advances. There is a unique infinite run, but no accepting computation consuming the whole input. This also fails to accept.

For acceptance, retain the course's end-of-input accepting-state condition; for a nonempty input, reaching an accepting state before consuming all symbols is insufficient. A failed or looping path is not transformed into success by the label “deterministic.” Later, when discussing Turing machines, we will distinguish explicitly rejecting from looping. Keep that distinction separate from the language-level fact that both are nonacceptance.

Suppose δ(q,0,ε)=(r,X): read 0, push X. Also suppose δ(q,ε,X)=(s,ε): pop X without reading. When the next input is 0 and the current top is X, both can run. An ε cycle may keep running without consuming the next input symbol. A configuration may become stuck when the input or stack is empty and its required action is unavailable. The four-way condition is stated over actual alphabet symbols; ε is the no-consumption option, not a real member of the input alphabet.

Acceptance is still about a computation that has consumed the whole input and is in an accepting state. Visiting an accepting state while unread input remains isn't enough. This is unlike a Turing machine's accepting halt state, which will be introduced later.

Why a PDA is not always convertible to an equivalent DPDA

DFA and NFA recognize the same languages. PDA and DPDA do not: deterministic context-free languages form a proper subclass of context-free languages. Why give a machine a stack?

For 0n1n0^n1^n0n1n, after reading n zeros the machine owes n matching ones. A DFA has only finitely many states, so it cannot keep a different state for every possible debt. What changes when the stack isn't enough?

A PDA can only inspect the top of its stack. A Turing machine, or TM, has a tape and a head that moves left or right. It can read a cell, overwrite it, move, and come back later. The finite state still stores the current phase of the program; the tape stores the growing work.

The tape has no fixed upper bound on usable cells. That does not mean infinitely many cells are processed in one move. At every finite point in a computation, only finitely much work has happened. “Unbounded workspace” and “infinite work for free” are completely different claims.

Now the machine gets a writable notebook. It can mark something, walk away, and come back to read the mark. That is a huge change from a stack that only exposes its top. But there is no teleporting: the head still moves cell by cell, and every mark needs a stated meaning.

Round 1 — The tape is a workspace you can revisit · pages 1–2

Finite automata retain a bounded state summary. A PDA adds an unbounded stack but only exposes its top. A Turing machine adds a tape whose cells can be read, overwritten and revisited by moving left or right. That's the new power: the machine can leave marks and come back to them later.

“Unrestricted memory” on the slide concerns access compared with a stack. A TM still has strict local mechanics: one head reads one cell, one transition writes one symbol and moves one cell. It cannot teleport to an arbitrary address or inspect infinitely many cells at once. The tape is unbounded in the model; each finite computation visits only finitely many cells.

Unlike DFA/PDA acceptance after the full input has been processed, entering q_accept or q_reject halts a TM immediately. A correctly designed TM must inspect what the language requires before choosing either state. The model does not force it to scan every original input cell first.

Round 2 — Same order is why this job needs revisiting · pages 3–4

The language {w#w:w∈{0,1}*} asks for two identical strings in the same order. For w=01, the accepted input is 01#01, not 01#10. A simple push-first-half/pop-second-half strategy naturally compares reverse order, which is the wrong job here.

The TM marks the next unmatched symbol on the left, remembers whether it was 0 or 1 in its finite state, crosses #, finds the next unmatched symbol on the right, compares and marks it, then returns left. Each round matches the corresponding position. Marks x mean “already handled,” not an extra input symbol supplied by the user.

Before accepting, check exactly one separator, legal input symbols, no unmatched left symbols and no extra unmatched right symbols. # accepts because w=ε is permitted. 0# and #0 reject because lengths differ. 01#00 rejects at the second comparison. These cases explain why merely finding one matching pair is insufficient.

For ua qᵢ bv, the scanned symbol is b, while a is immediately to its left. If δ(qᵢ,b)=(qⱼ,c,L), write c where b was and move left: u qⱼ acv. For a right move, obtain uac qⱼ v. Separate the write action from the head movement; doing only one produces the wrong configuration.

A finite sequence of legal configurations from q₀w to an accepting configuration proves that M accepts w. A recognizer must eventually accept members, but on nonmembers it may reject or loop. A decider must halt on every input, accepting members and rejecting nonmembers. “Hasn't accepted yet” cannot tell you whether a recognizer will reject later or run forever.

Round 2 — The copy-checker's two leftward phases are different jobs · pages 6–10

For w#w, q₁ selects and marks the next left-half bit. The 0 branch uses q₂ then q₄ to cross the separator and demand a matching right-half 0. The 1 branch uses q₃ then q₅ and demands 1. The state remembers which bit was selected while the head travels.

After marking the right bit, q₆ travels left through the right half toward #. q₇ then travels through the remaining unmarked left bits until finding the marked left prefix, and moves right to the next work position. They cannot be merged: x means “keep going left” in the right half, but “turn around here” at the left prefix boundary. Same scanned symbol, different phase, different action.

For 01#01, the completed marking rounds are 01#01 → x1#x1 → xx#xx. q₈ verifies there are only x's in the remainder of the right half, then accepts at blank. Missing rejecting edges in the slide diagram are shorthand for rejection, not permission to improvise a transition.

Round 3 — Multiplication is repeated complete matching · pages 11–12

The source language is {aⁱbʲcᵏ:ij=k, i,j,k≥1}. First check nonempty blocks in that exact order. For each a, traverse all b's and cancel one previously uncancelled c per b. Restore the b markers after that round so the next a can use the full b count again. Mark that a as completed.

For aabbcccc, two a-rounds each cancel two c's, totaling four. For aabbccc, the second round runs out of c's; reject. For aabbccccc, all rounds finish but one c remains; reject. Empty blocks reject because the source specifies positive exponents.

A subroutine is a previously explained finite-state/tape procedure used as part of a larger machine, such as returning to a marker or matching a selected symbol. A high-level description is justified when these operations are implementable with the allowed head moves and finite work alphabet. It cannot hide an impossible instruction such as “instantly know whether this computation will ever stop.”

Round 4 — Pairwise means every different pair, not just neighbors · pages 13–14

The distinct-block language requires xᵢ≠xⱼ whenever i≠j. Adjacent comparisons alone miss duplicates in positions 1 and 3. Mark the two selected separators, compare their following blocks, restore temporary work, and advance through pairs (1,2),(1,3),…,(2,3),… . There are ℓ(ℓ−1)/2 pairs for ℓ blocks.

Blocks may be empty because each xᵢ belongs to {0,1}*. Two empty blocks are duplicates. Their delimiters still identify their positions. The slide suggests copying the selected blocks to spare tape and using a same-order comparison there; preserve the originals or restore them before later comparisons.

Correctness: reject when a checked pair is equal; accept only after every pair was checked and found different. Termination: finitely many pairs, finite-length comparisons, and a pair index that advances. The proof must account for both exhaustive coverage and finite progress.

This is why copying only the state sequence is not a full TM trace. You need the tape edits and head movement too.

Three possible outcomes, not two

A TM may accept, reject, or keep running forever. A recognizer for L must accept every member and must never accept a nonmember. On a nonmember it may reject or loop. A decider must halt on every input, accepting members and rejecting nonmembers.

Every decider is a recognizer. Calling a recognizer “an algorithm that answers yes or no” overstates its guarantee: on some no-instances it may never answer. You cannot turn it into a decider by getting impatient after a million steps; a genuine accepting run might take a million and one.

Read the deck's constructions as phase programs

The w#w machine alternates marking, crossing, comparing, and returning. Its return states need different jobs on the two sides of #; reaching a marked symbol on the right is not the same as reaching the last processed symbol on the left. First translate the exact task

Input has the form text#mask#. The text uses a,b; the mask uses 0,1; their lengths are promised equal. Keep a text character if its corresponding mask bit is 1. Append the kept characters after the second #. The problem permits erasing processed text and mask positions and requires at most eleven states excluding reject.

Example: ab#01# must append b. The a pairs with 0 and is discarded; the b pairs with 1 and is copied. This is not a recognizer checking equal strings; it is a transducer computing an output.

Why blank means different things in different phases

At q₃/q₄ a blank is a processed mask bit, so scan right. At q₅/q₆, after the current bit, there are no processed mask blanks ahead: remaining mask bits, separator, then output are nonblank. The first blank is the output end. At q₉, the first blank reached from the right is the processed-text boundary, so turn right. The state tells you which meaning is relevant. Without the phase, the same blank symbol is ambiguous.

Correctness and halting

After k rounds, the first k text symbols and first k mask bits are erased. The output equals exactly the kept characters among those k positions, in their original order. Remaining text and mask suffixes are unchanged. In round k+1, q₀ remembers the next text character, q₃/q₄ finds the corresponding first unprocessed mask bit, and the branch either appends that character or skips it. The return phases restore the head to the next text symbol. Hence the invariant holds for k+1.

When all text is erased, q₀ reads the first # and accepts. Equal promised lengths ensure all mask positions have also been processed. Each round processes one pair; every scan crosses a finite region to a guaranteed boundary. Therefore the machine halts. The empty case ## accepts with empty output.

The job: replace convenient hardware with an encoding

A multitape machine has k tapes and k head positions. A single tape can store their finite used contents as separated blocks, for example #block₁#block₂#…#blockₖ#. Use a marked version of a symbol to indicate each simulated head's position. The delimiter and marked symbols are extra members of the simulator's finite tape alphabet.

The representation must retain both the data and the head locations. Merely joining the tape contents loses which cell each simulated head reads next. That's the same lesson as configurations: an encoding has to preserve everything needed to determine the next step.

Put pictures of several notebooks into one long file, with separators between them. Mark one virtual cursor in each picture. The real head scans that file and updates the pictures. That is the simulation: several imagined heads represented by data, maintained by one actual moving head.

Round 1 — One tape holds a serialized picture of all the tapes · pages 1–3

Imagine putting several short documents into one file separated by dividers. A delimiter # separates virtual tape regions. A dotted version of a symbol marks each virtual head. The encoding in the slide stores three tape contents in sequence, with exactly one head mark per region.

A dotted 0 is a distinct tape symbol whose meaning is “this cell contains 0 and its virtual head is here.” It does not change the simulated data value to some mysterious number. If the original alphabet is finite, adding one marked copy of each symbol and finitely many separators still gives a finite alphabet.

The simulator has one physical head. The other head positions are data recorded on its tape. This distinction is the entire trick: it represents the multitape configuration rather than physically possessing those extra heads.

Round 2 — One virtual step takes several physical sweeps · page 4

First scan the encoding to collect the symbols under the k marked heads. Since k and Γ are fixed for this machine, there are only finitely many possible scanned-symbol tuples; the finite control can remember which tuple it saw. Apply the simulated transition rule to determine the new state, writes and directions.

Then sweep through the regions to rewrite the marked cells and move each marker one position. Preserve separators and all untouched simulated cells. The simulator may use intermediate marker types to distinguish old and updated head positions, so it does not accidentally perform the same virtual update twice.

If a virtual head moves beyond the represented right end, extend that region with a blank cell. That may require shifting later regions right. The original tape is infinite, so extra physical space exists; the simulation still performs a finite number of operations for this one step.

Round 3 — How can finite control move an arbitrarily long suffix? · page 5

The control need not remember the whole suffix. It carries just one displaced symbol at a time. To open a cell, replace its current symbol with the desired new one and remember the displaced symbol in the state. Move right, write the carried symbol, remember what was displaced there, and repeat. When the end of the finite represented region is reached, write the final carried symbol into spare space.

For the slide's binary shifting illustration, states q₀ and q₁ mean “carrying 0” and “carrying 1.” For a larger finite alphabet, use one carrying state per symbol, including separators and head-marked symbols. The carrying state stores a symbol value, not an unbounded string. That is why the construction remains a finite-state controller.

A delimiter marks region structure; a blank can be legitimate simulated tape content. A robust full encoding keeps track of the represented region's end so shifting does not confuse an internal simulated blank with the end of all data.

Round 4 — What the simulation proof must preserve

After each completed simulated step, the encoding must describe exactly the original machine's state, every represented tape symbol and every virtual head position. Initially we can construct the encoding from the input and blank auxiliary tapes. Each sweep implements the corresponding old transition, so induction preserves the representation.

If the multitape machine accepts or rejects, the simulator eventually reaches that corresponding halting state. If it runs forever, the simulator keeps producing faithful steps. For a decider, every source computation is finite and every simulated step uses finitely many physical moves, so the simulator also halts. None of this says one virtual step costs one physical step; complexity analysis later counts that overhead.

Simulate one joint step in phases

First sweep across the encoded blocks and read each marked symbol. Remember the k-symbol tuple in the simulator's finite control. This is possible because k and the simulated tape alphabet are fixed for this machine, so there are only finitely many possible tuples.

Use the simulated transition function to determine the new state, writes, and movements. Make another sweep to update each marked cell and move the head marker to its neighbor. Different blocks may move their heads in different directions, but all updates come from the same old snapshot.

For example, if the simulated heads read (0,1) and the rule writes (x,0) with movements (R,L), the simulator must find those two marked cells, replace their contents, and move the two markers appropriately. It must not let the new x influence the choice of the second write.

What if a simulated tape needs more room?

If a head moves past its block's currently represented end, insert a blank cell into that block. Shift later encoded cells right to make space. A finite-control carry can move symbols one by one; this is tedious but implementable. The block remains a faithful representation of a tape with additional blank cells available.

The simulation is allowed to use many real steps for one simulated step. Computability asks whether the same answers can be produced at all, not whether the hardware has identical speed.

The invariant is the proof

At the beginning of each simulated step, the single-tape encoding represents exactly the multitape machine's complete configuration. Initialization establishes this. Reading the stored head tuple and applying the same transition preserves it. Therefore each finite accepting or rejecting computation is reproduced, and an endless simulated computation continues rather than being falsely turned into a halt.

Conversely, a multitape machine can ignore extra tapes and behave like a one-tape machine. The two simulations establish equal recognition and decision power.

Why this matters later

We can design algorithms using convenient multiple tapes, then know a standard TM can implement them. In complexity theory we revisit this exact construction and count its cost: scanning and shifting a growing representation can turn t simulated steps into roughly quadratic work. Same computability is compatible with different efficiency.

Keep: encode complete configurations; preserve them one simulated step at a time. The proof is an invariant plus a way to implement each update.

Two tapes: one simultaneous move

A k-tape transition reads k symbols, writes k symbols, and moves k heads, while changing one shared control state. For two tapes,

δ(q,a,b)=(r,c,d,R,L)\delta(q,a,b)=(r,c,d,R,L)δ(q,a,b)=(r,c,d,R,L)

reads a on tape 1 and b on tape 2, writes c,d respectively, moves head 1 right and head 2 left, and enters r. Do not run the tape-1 line as one machine step and the tape-2 line as another. They are one joint instruction.

The course allows a stay move S for multitape machines. The input starts on tape 1; all other tapes are blank. Any extra left marker you want must be a permitted symbol, and its initialization must be part of the construction.

A concrete copy routine

Suppose the tape alphabet permits a marker $. First write $ at the beginning of tape 2 and move its head right, leaving tape 1 at the first input character. For each a∈{0,1}, read a on tape 1 and blank on tape 2; write a on tape 2 and move both heads right. At tape-1 blank, the copy is complete. Move tape-2 head left over copied symbols until $, then right. Now tape 2 is positioned at the copy's first symbol.

This illustrates initialization, simultaneous updates, and boundary detection. It is not a substitute for a past question that restricts Γ={0,1,#,⊔} and forbids $. Under such a restriction you must use the permitted marking strategy or boundary behavior.

Simulating many tapes with one

Encode each tape's used region as a block separated by #. Mark the scanned cell in each block using a dotted version of its symbol. A one-tape simulator scans the blocks to learn the k scanned symbols, remembers that finite tuple in its control, then scans again to perform the prescribed writes and move the marks. If a simulated head needs a fresh cell, insert a blank in that block and shift later material right.

For fixed k and finite Γ, the tuple of scanned symbols has finitely many possibilities, so finite control can remember it. Each simulated step takes finitely many scans. This proves simulation of arbitrary computations, not just a sample trace. The reverse direction is immediate: a one-tape machine is a special case of a multitape machine.

A stay move can also be simulated: write the requested symbol, move right into an intermediate state, then move left while preserving that next cell. Choosing right then left avoids problems at the left boundary.

Nondeterministic TM: why breadth-first matters

An NTM accepts if at least one branch accepts. A depth-first simulator may follow one infinite branch forever while another branch accepts after five steps. So enumerate the tree fairly, by depth: all paths of length 0, then 1, then 2, and so on. Each layer is finite because each configuration has finitely many next moves.

If an accepting branch exists, it has a finite length d, and the breadth-first simulator eventually visits it. Thus a deterministic TM can recognize the same language. This is not NFA subset construction into finitely many control states; TM configurations include arbitrarily long tape contents.

If every branch halts on every input, finite branching implies the computation tree for a fixed input is finite (the relevant consequence of König's lemma). Then exhaustive breadth-first simulation can also reject after all branches finish. This establishes equivalence for deciders too. “Every branch is finite” alone would not imply a finite tree if infinite branching were allowed; finite branching is essential.

Fresh practice

An NTM's left branch loops forever; its right branch accepts after three steps. Describe what a depth-first simulator and a breadth-first simulator might do, and whether the NTM accepts.

Show the answer

The NTM accepts because its right branch accepts. A depth-first simulator choosing the left branch first can loop forever and never discover this. Breadth-first explores all finite depths fairly, so it eventually reaches the right branch's accepting configuration at depth three. This explains recognizer equivalence; it does not make the original NTM a decider, because one branch fails to halt.

The whole-course question gets bigger

We've shown that several formal machine designs have the same computability power. Now connect them to the informal word algorithm: a finite, mechanical procedure whose individual steps can be carried out effectively. The Church–Turing thesis says that effectively computable procedures are captured by Turing machines.

This is a thesis linking an informal notion to a formal model, not a theorem proved by manipulating two formal definitions. Equivalence between one-tape and multitape TMs is a theorem; the claim that the model captures every effective method has a different logical status.

Searching for a witness is like checking lockers for a missing key. Finding it settles yes. Checking a million empty lockers does not settle no when infinitely many remain. A proved finite search bound changes the job completely: exhaust that bounded region and you can finally justify rejection.

Round 1 — “Find an algorithm” needs a definition of what counts · pages 1–4

Hilbert's tenth problem asks for a method that takes an integer-coefficient polynomial and decides whether some integer assignment makes it zero. An integer root is such an assignment; with several variables it is a tuple of integers. A polynomial is a finite sum of coefficient-times-variable-power terms.

The source example 6x³yz²+3xy²−x³−10 becomes zero at x=5,y=3,z=0: the first term is 0, the second 135, the third −125, and the last −10. Checking one proposed assignment is finite arithmetic. Deciding that no assignment exists is the harder issue.

The Church–Turing thesis identifies effectively executable algorithms with Turing-computable procedures. Because “effectively executable” began as an informal concept, this is not a theorem comparing two independently formalized mathematical definitions. The slide calls it a definition; thesis emphasizes the proposed identification supported by equivalent computational models. It is not a theorem that every physical phenomenon can be simulated efficiently.

Round 2 — Search can recognize without deciding · pages 5–8

For one variable, try 0,1,−1,2,−2,… . If evaluation gives zero, accept. Every integer eventually gets tried, so a root will eventually be found. Without an additional stopping rule, a rootless polynomial makes this search continue forever.

A proven finite root bound changes the job. If every root lies in [−B,B], test only those finitely many integers and reject after they all fail. For the source's 4x³−2x²+x−7, its stated bound gives B=4×7/4=7. Use the absolute value of the leading coefficient when writing a general positive bound; a negative leading coefficient cannot give a negative search radius. Treat the zero polynomial and nonzero constants separately.

Why can a one-variable bound work? Far enough from zero, the highest-degree term dominates the sum of lower-degree terms, so the polynomial cannot cancel to zero. If degree is d, there are k nonzero terms, maximum coefficient magnitude c_max and leading magnitude |c_d|, then for |x|>max(1,k c_max/|c_d|), the leading magnitude exceeds the total lower-degree magnitude. This gives a sufficient finite radius, even if not the tightest one.

The general multivariable problem is undecidable. The slide's “bounds are not possible” should be read as absence of a general computable search bound sufficient for deciding all inputs, not a claim that every particular polynomial lacks bounded solutions. That impossibility is a deep result cited by the deck, not proved by the little enumeration example.

Round 3 — Three levels of describing the same algorithm · pages 9–10

High-level: say what logical operations are performed, such as compare every pair. Implementation-level: explain tape marking, scans, stored phase and restoration. Formal-level: provide states and transitions. A high-level description is acceptable when its operations are known to be implementable and the required level permits it. Round 4 — Connectivity is reachability from one chosen node · pages 11–13

An undirected graph is connected when every vertex can be reached from every other through edges. Choose one vertex, mark it, then repeatedly mark any vertex adjacent to a marked one. Stop when a complete pass marks no new vertex. Accept if every vertex is marked; otherwise reject.

Why correct? Every newly marked vertex has a path from the chosen start, extending the path to its marked neighbor. Conversely, if a reachable vertex remained unmarked at the fixed point, take a path to it and the first unmarked vertex on that path; its predecessor is marked, so it should have been marked too. Contradiction. Termination follows because each productive pass adds a vertex and the graph is finite.

⟨G⟩ means an encoding of graph G as a string. Validate that encoding before graph reasoning: node identifiers are distinct and edge endpoints refer to listed nodes. The displayed tuple/list format is one representation, not the graph itself. Mathematical objects become TM inputs only after such a finite encoding is specified.

Searching for an integer root: yes can be easier than no

Given a polynomial with integer coefficients, try integer inputs and evaluate it. For one variable, test 0,1,−1,2,−2,… . If an integer root exists, this enumeration eventually finds it. If none exists, an unbounded search alone runs forever. It is a recognizer-style strategy for the yes-instances, not automatically a decider.

For a univariate polynomial of positive degree with nonzero leading coefficient, a computable bound on root magnitude turns this into a finite search. Once every integer within a valid bound has been checked, there are no other possible integer roots to miss. For the slide example 4x3−2x2+x−74x^3-2x^2+x-74x3−2x2+x−7, a bound such as |x|≤7 suffices. Constant and zero polynomials are handled separately rather than dividing by a nonexistent leading term.

The critical extra ingredient is the stopping certificate supplied by the bound. “I haven't found a root yet” is not the same information as “I've exhausted every possible root.”

Why the multivariable result is deeper

For polynomial equations in several integer variables, enumerating all integer tuples still recognizes existence of a solution. Hilbert's tenth problem asked for a general deciding procedure. The negative result says no such algorithm exists for all instances. This does not follow just from the fact that our current brute-force search may loop; a bad algorithm doesn't prove all algorithms impossible. An impossibility theorem needs a reduction or other general argument.

Three levels of describing a TM

A formal description lists states and transitions. An implementation-level description explains tape layouts, head movements, and marking. A high-level description uses algorithmic steps such as “search the finite graph,” while making those steps effective and termination clear.

For graph connectivity, begin with one marked vertex, repeatedly mark neighbors of marked vertices, and stop when a whole pass adds nothing. A finite graph has only finitely many vertices to add. Accept if all are marked. The algorithm's monotone progress explains termination; the reachability invariant explains correctness.

An input encoding, written with angle brackets such as ⟨G⟩, turns an abstract object into a finite string. Invalid encodings must have specified treatment. The machine reads symbols, not a Platonic graph floating in the void.

Finite automata: finite state only. DFA, NFA, and regular expressions describe the regular languages. An NFA can branch but does not gain language-recognition power over a DFA.

Pushdown automata: finite control plus one unbounded stack. Nondeterministic PDAs and CFGs describe the context-free languages. Every regular language is context-free: a PDA can simulate a DFA while ignoring its stack. Some context-free languages, such as 0ⁿ1ⁿ, are not regular. DPDAs recognize a proper subclass of CFLs.

Turing machines: rewritable tape with a moving head. Multitape and nondeterministic variants have the same recognition power as ordinary deterministic TMs. Deciders halt on every input; recognizers may loop on nonmembers. The ability to simulate another model establishes containment; separating examples establish that a containment is strict.

The published course explicitly omits the detailed non-context-free-language discussion at the end of PDA 5. CFL pumping was absent as a direct task in the six inspected Midterm 2 papers. It is not part of this site's core practice route unless current class information adds it. Regular-language pumping is a separate theorem and must not be used to prove a language is not context-free.

Regular-language closure: what “closed” means

A class is closed under an operation if applying that operation to languages in the class always produces another language in the class. “Open” is not the usual opposite term here; say “not closed.” One result being regular does not establish closure of the whole class.

Inputs regular Result Construction / proof idea
A,B A∪B regular NFA with fresh ε-branching start
A,B A∩B regular Product DFA tracks both states; accept when both accept
A,B AB regular NFA ε-links from A's finals to B's start
A A* regular Fresh accepting start; enter A and repeat from its finals

A,B A−B regular Intersect A with complement of B

For the product, after reading w the state is (state of M_A after w, state of M_B after w). This invariant holds initially and is preserved by each paired transition. The accepting pair condition therefore implements intersection exactly. For union, accept if either component accepts. Same transitions, changed final set.

Do not complement an NFA by flipping circles: existential acceptance does not become universal rejection that way. Convert to a complete DFA first.

Regular expressions and GNFA elimination

RE operations are union, concatenation and star. ε denotes the language {ε}; ∅ denotes the empty language. Therefore Rε=R, R∅=∅, R∪∅=R, and ∅*=ε. Concatenation order matters.

Both DFA→GNFA and NFA→GNFA can be done directly. Add a new start with an ε edge to the old start, a new sole accept reached by ε from old finals, union parallel edges, and label missing edges ∅. An NFA's symbol and ε labels are already regular expressions. Subset construction is not required for this route.

When removing state k, update each remaining i→j label to

Rij ∪ Rik(Rkk)∗Rkj.R_{ij}\ \cup\ R_{ik}(R_{kk})^*R_{kj}.Rij​ ∪ Rik​(Rkk​)∗Rkj​.

The old edge handles paths bypassing k. The new term enters k, loops there zero or more times, then leaves. This explains the star and the order. Update from the old labels for that elimination step, not labels partly overwritten during the same step.

A proof template that travels

For any conversion, write: what does a state/variable/configuration represent; why is that true at the start; how is it preserved by each move/rule; why does the ending condition correspond to membership? For a decider, add why the process halts. For equivalence, cover both directions. For impossibility, identify a property forced by every candidate machine and construct an input that violates it.

The Church–Turing thesis connects the informal notion of an effective algorithm with Turing computation. It is a thesis about that informal notion, not a mathematical theorem proved by checking a few programs. The course's Hilbert deck uses integer-root search to contrast a recognizer with a bounded terminating decision procedure.

Fresh practice

Someone claims: “A and B are nonregular, so A∪B must be nonregular.” Disprove this using a language L and its complement over the same alphabet, and explain why both can be nonregular.

Show the counterexample

Take L={0ⁿ1ⁿ | n≥0}, which is nonregular, and its complement over {0,1}*. The complement is also nonregular: if it were regular, closure of regular languages under complement would make L regular, a contradiction. Yet their union is {0,1}*, which is regular. Closure promises concern inputs inside the class; they do not give this claimed rule for nonregular inputs.

Repair the star example without changing its language

Keep the base language fixed as A={01}. Its NFA is u —0→ v —1→ w, with u starting and w accepting. To recognize A*, add a fresh accepting start s, an ε arrow s→u, and an ε arrow w→u. Keep w accepting. No other arrows exist.

State 0 1 ε Accepting?
s ∅ ∅ {u} Yes
u {v} ∅ ∅ No
v ∅ {w} ∅ No
w ∅ ∅ {u} Yes

The fresh s accepts ε. A completed 01 finishes at w and may begin another complete copy through u. The strings 01 and 0101 accept; 001 and 01001 reject. There is no zero self-loop here. Adding a zero self-loop at u would make 001 possible; that would change the language and break this construction. The accepting new start does not grant arbitrary extra input a free pass: some path still has to consume the entire input.

How to study with this book

Aight, the goal is to make the mechanism runnable in your head. The explanation should let you predict what happens next and justify why, then give you a real chance to do it yourself. Slang is the voice; worked reasoning, feedback and later retrieval do the learning work.

Reading an explanation and producing a proof from a blank page are different skills. This workbook trains both. The target is strong performance on unfamiliar exam questions, including exact procedures and complete justification.

Start with a model you can run in your head

For a new deck, first identify the question it solves and the objects involved. Then follow one complete example with the changing information visible. Connect each symbol to that example, explain why each step preserves the intended meaning, and reconnect the result to the next concept. The 49 deck guides and complete mental-model edition follow this structure. The worked lessons now begin with the relevant model too.

A mental model earns its keep when it lets you predict a new case and explain a changed condition. “A stack is a to-do list” is only the entry point: you must also know top-first order, what each transition changes, and what counts as completion. Analogies are explicitly mapped to the mathematics and stopped where they stop helping.

This sequence applies the worked-example, explicit-teaching, concrete-to-abstract and proof-explanation guidance cited below. It is not a newly validated universal method. The conversational language is a learner preference; the learning claims come from actual attempts and later recall, not the number of slang words or pages read.

The daily loop

  1. Retrieve before rereading. Start with one previously studied task. Write what you remember on paper. Then check it.
  2. Learn one complete example. Read the problem, its purpose, each transition or proof step, and why that step is justified. Follow the linked slide pages when the original diagram matters.
  3. Close it and produce. Solve a related task without copying the example. If you need help, use the hint; record the attempt as assisted.
  4. Repair the first broken link. Was it notation, the mechanism, the procedure, choosing a method, or proving the claim? Relearn that link, then attempt a different example.
  5. Return after a delay. Aim for the next day, roughly three days later, then a week later. These are adjustable defaults, not a scientifically optimal schedule. Mix related topics after you can attempt their basic procedures.

The daily page suggests one lesson and shows locally due practice. It doesn't turn a click or a streak into a claim of mastery. Your attempt history distinguishes needs work, assisted, independent once, and later recall. These are self-reports; this static site does not grade mathematical proofs.

Why this design

Worked examples and explicit modelling reduce the amount a novice must invent at once. Guidance should then fade into independent work rather than remain a permanent stream of answers. The AERO guides and the worked-example meta-analysis support that sequence, with important differences across learners and tasks. AERO explicit teaching, AERO scaffolding, Barbieri et al., 2023.

Retrieval practice and spacing generally improve later access to knowledge. Trying to reconstruct a proof or diagram can reveal gaps that rereading hides. Transfer to changed problems needs deliberate practice too; recalling a familiar sentence is not enough. Agarwal et al., 2021, Carpenter et al., 2022, Latimier et al., 2021.

The latest review checked here, published October 29, 2026, finds classroom benefits of retrieval, spacing and interleaving, with variation across contexts and limited evidence for some moderators. Those are population-level findings, not a promise about one exam or an optimal schedule for one student. Ruitenburg et al., 2026.

For proofs, ask what licenses a crucial step and how it connects to the claim. Research on self-explanation training supports proof comprehension; it does not establish that reading explained proofs automatically produces independent proof-writing skill. This is why the workbook has both a complete proof and a fresh task. Hodds, Alcock & Inglis, 2014.

Feedback should identify the error, the violated condition, and the next correction. “Wrong” and “great job” alone give little direction. Wisniewski et al., 2020. Explanations are useful when they connect reasons; demanding an explanation of every tiny step can add load. Rittle-Johnson, 2024. Concrete examples and formal statements should be explicitly connected. IES practice guide.

AI can help during practice while leaving independent performance weaker if it supplies answers too freely. One high-school mathematics field experiment found this risk and evidence that tutoring safeguards mitigated it. That result motivates hidden solutions and independent retests here; it does not diagnose any particular student's result. Bastani et al., 2025.

Stanford CS103 separates creative problem-solving from formal proof writing, explicitly teaches assumptions and conclusions, and checks variable scope and justified claims. The strongest practical combination here is accurate modelling, meaningful independent attempts, specific feedback, delayed retrieval, and varied exam tasks. A practical study block

Use about 45–60 minutes as a starting structure: 5–10 minutes recalling an earlier task, 15–20 minutes following one worked example, 15–20 minutes attempting and checking a fresh problem, and a few minutes recording the next repair. These times are planning choices. If a concept breaks, spend the block repairing it; reading more pages doesn't compensate for a missing dependency.

For daily reading on a phone, study the explanation and trace a small example. For exam practice, use paper and close the lesson. During a mixed mock, set the timer, keep solutions shut, and review only after the block. Use the attempt record to note solution exposure. Problem 1 · Grammar and normal form · 25 points

Let Σ={a,b}, V={S,A,B}, and

S → AB | A
A → aA | a
B → bB | ε

(a) Give a leftmost derivation of aab. (4)

(b) Prove this grammar is ambiguous using aa. Give two leftmost derivations. (6)

(c) Convert to CNF. Show a new start, ε elimination, unit elimination, and final helper rules. (15)

Review Problem 1 after your attempt

(a) S⇒AB⇒aAB⇒aaB⇒aabB⇒aab. (b) S⇒A⇒aA⇒aa, and S⇒AB⇒aAB⇒aaB⇒aa. The start choices differ and both derivations are leftmost.

(c) Add S₀→S. Only B is nullable. Removing B→ε adds S→A (already present) and B→b; the grammar now has S→AB|A, A→aA|a, B→bB|b. Eliminate units S→A and S₀→S: S₀→AB|aA|a; S→AB|aA|a; A→aA|a; B→bB|b. Introduce U→a and W→b: S₀→AB|UA|a; S→AB|UA|a; A→UA|a; B→WB|b; U→a; W→b. No start ε rule is permitted: the original language contains at least one a.

Rubric: 4 for a valid leftmost derivation; 6 for two distinct leftmost derivations of the same string and the conclusion. CNF: new start 2, correct nullable alternatives and ε removal 4, unit elimination 4, legal final shapes 3, all stages shown 2. A language-changing omission needs correction even if a few strings still derive.

Problem 2 · Build and justify a PDA · 25 points

Construct a PDA for L={0m1n2n∣m≥0,n≥1}L=\{0^m1^n2^n\mid m\geq0,n\geq1\}L={0m1n2n∣m≥0,n≥1} with at most five states and Γ={X,$}. Give its states, start, accepting set, transitions, and a proof of both directions. Trace 01122 through acceptance, and explain why 002 is rejected.

Review Problem 2 after your attempt

Use Q={s,p,r,t,f}, start s, F={f}, Σ={0,1,2}. Moves: s —ε,ε→$→ p; p —0,ε→ε→ p; p —1,ε→X→ r; r —1,ε→X→ r; r —2,X→ε→ t; t —2,X→ε→ t; t —ε,$→ε→ f. All unspecified δ entries are ∅; each listed PDA output is a singleton pair set.

Every accepting run consumes only zeros in p, at least one 1 to reach r, and at least one 2 to reach t. X markers count unmatched ones; reaching f requires all removed. Thus every accepted string has the required form and counts. Conversely, every such string can take m zero loops, n pushes, n pops, and the bottom-marker finish.

Trace: (s,01122,ε)→(p,01122,$)→(p,1122,$)→(r,122,X$)→(r,22,XX$)→(t,2,X$)→(t,ε,$)→(f,ε,ε). For 002, after the zeros the machine remains in p with next symbol 2, for which there is no move. It rejects.

Rubric: correct construction under constraints 10; formal components and transition types 4; soundness and completeness 6; full accepting trace 3; rejection explanation 2. Recheck ε and inputs with no ones: n≥1 is essential.

Problem 3 · PDA-to-CFG conversion · 20 points

A normalized PDA has states {s,p,r,f}, start s, sole accept f, and transitions:

s — ε,ε→$ → p
p — a,ε→X → p
p — b,X→ε → r
r — b,X→ε → r
r — ε,$→ε → f

(a) State why the normalization conditions hold. (4)

(b) Define the variables and start variable, give every wrapped rule with a pairing table, and specify the other two rule families. (12)

(c) Derive aabb from the start variable. (4)

Review Problem 3 after your attempt

(a) f is the only accepting state, and its only entrance pops $ after all X markers. Every transition pushes or pops exactly one symbol. (b) Variables Aᵢⱼ for i,j∈{s,p,r,f}; start Aₛf. Pair $ push s→p with pop r→f to get Aₛf→Aₚr. Pair X push p→p on a with pop p→r on b to get Aₚr→aAₚpb; pair it with pop r→r on b to get Aₚr→aAₚrb. Other families: Aᵢⱼ→AᵢₖAₖⱼ for all i,j,k and Aᵢᵢ→ε for all i. (c) Aₛf⇒Aₚr⇒aAₚrb⇒aaAₚpbb⇒aabb.

Rubric: normalization 4; variables/start 3, three correct pairings 6, two remaining families 3; valid derivation 4. A wrong inner index changes the meaning even if the surrounding terminals look right.

Problem 4 · Turing-machine construction · 20 points

Input is promised to be w# followed by blanks, where w is a nonempty binary string. Compute the bitwise complement of w in place, leave # unchanged, and halt immediately to the right of #. Use only L/R moves and at most two working states, plus accept and reject. Give a complete transition table over Γ={0,1,#,⊔}, trace 01#, and justify halting. Explain what changes if the input promise is removed and ε must be rejected.

Review Problem 4 after your attempt

Under the promise, one working state q suffices: on 0 write 1,R,q; on 1 write 0,R,q; on # write #,R,accept; on blank write blank,R,reject. Trace: q01#⊔⇒1q1#⊔⇒10q#⊔⇒10#q_accept⊔. After k scanned bits, exactly the first k bits are complemented and the remainder unchanged. Each move goes right, so the finite input reaches # and halts.

Without the promise, specify the desired valid format first. If it must be nonempty binary w followed by exactly one # and then blank, use a separate initial state requiring 0 or 1, and a state after # to check blank. A straightforward strict validator uses three working states: initial, scan, and check-after-separator. This is a construction, not a minimality claim; do not assume the original two-state constraint is automatically retained. The question asks what changes, not to force an unjustified minimal state count.

Rubric: table with correct writes/head movement 8; entire trace 4; invariant and halting 5; promise/validation distinction 3. The trace alone does not establish correctness for all promised inputs.

Problem 5 · Pumping with a changed boundary · 10 points

Prove L={0m1n∣m>2n, n≥1}L=\{0^m1^n\mid m>2n,\ n\geq1\}L={0m1n∣m>2n, n≥1} is not regular. Explicitly give the witness string and pumping repetition, and cover every legal split.

Review Problem 5 after your attempt

Suppose L is regular with pumping length p≥1. Choose s=0²ᵖ⁺¹1ᵖ∈L, of length 3p+1≥p. For every split s=xyz with |xy|≤p and |y|>0, y=0ᵏ for some 1≤k≤p. Choose i=0. Then xy⁰z=0²ᵖ⁺¹⁻ᵏ1ᵖ has at most 2p zeros and p ones, violating m>2n. This contradicts pumping, so L is nonregular.

Rubric: assumption/arbitrary p 1; valid long witness 2; all-splits argument 3; specific pumping count and failed condition 3; contradiction/conclusion 1. An argument choosing a single convenient split is incomplete, even if its arithmetic is right.
