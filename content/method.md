Reading an explanation and producing a proof from a blank page are different skills. This workbook trains both. The target is strong performance on unfamiliar exam questions, including exact procedures and complete justification.

## Start with a model you can run in your head

For a new deck, first identify the question it solves and the objects involved. Then follow one complete example with the changing information visible. Connect each symbol to that example, explain why each step preserves the intended meaning, and reconnect the result to the next concept. The [49 deck guides](./#models) and [complete mental-model edition](mental-models.html) follow this structure. The worked lessons now begin with the relevant model too.

A mental model earns its keep when it lets you predict a new case and explain a changed condition. “A stack is a to-do list” is only the entry point: you must also know top-first order, what each transition changes, and what counts as completion. Analogies are explicitly mapped to the mathematics and stopped where they stop helping.

This sequence applies the worked-example, explicit-teaching, concrete-to-abstract and proof-explanation guidance cited below. It is not a newly validated universal method. The conversational language is a learner preference; the learning claims come from actual attempts and later recall, not the number of slang words or pages read.

## The daily loop

1. **Retrieve before rereading.** Start with one previously studied task. Write what you remember on paper. Then check it.
2. **Learn one complete example.** Read the problem, its purpose, each transition or proof step, and why that step is justified. Follow the linked slide pages when the original diagram matters.
3. **Close it and produce.** Solve a related task without copying the example. If you need help, use the hint; record the attempt as assisted.
4. **Repair the first broken link.** Was it notation, the mechanism, the procedure, choosing a method, or proving the claim? Relearn that link, then attempt a different example.
5. **Return after a delay.** Aim for the next day, roughly three days later, then a week later. These are adjustable defaults, not a scientifically optimal schedule. Mix related topics after you can attempt their basic procedures.

The daily page suggests one lesson and shows locally due practice. It doesn't turn a click or a streak into a claim of mastery. Your attempt history distinguishes **needs work**, **assisted**, **independent once**, and **later recall**. These are self-reports; this static site does not grade mathematical proofs.

## Why this design

Worked examples and explicit modelling reduce the amount a novice must invent at once. Guidance should then fade into independent work rather than remain a permanent stream of answers. The AERO guides and the worked-example meta-analysis support that sequence, with important differences across learners and tasks. [AERO explicit teaching](https://www.edresearch.edu.au/sites/default/files/2024-02/teach-explicitly-aa.pdf), [AERO scaffolding](https://www.edresearch.edu.au/sites/default/files/2024-11/AERO-practice-guide-scaffold-practice-aa.pdf), [Barbieri et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1).

Retrieval practice and spacing generally improve later access to knowledge. Trying to reconstruct a proof or diagram can reveal gaps that rereading hides. Transfer to changed problems needs deliberate practice too; recalling a familiar sentence is not enough. [Agarwal et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-021-09595-9), [Carpenter et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44159-022-00089-1), [Latimier et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8).

The latest review checked here, published October 29, 2026, finds classroom benefits of retrieval, spacing and interleaving, with variation across contexts and limited evidence for some moderators. Those are population-level findings, not a promise about one exam or an optimal schedule for one student. [Ruitenburg et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-026-10225-5).

For proofs, ask what licenses a crucial step and how it connects to the claim. Research on self-explanation training supports proof comprehension; it does not establish that reading explained proofs automatically produces independent proof-writing skill. This is why the workbook has both a complete proof and a fresh task. [Hodds, Alcock & Inglis, 2014](https://doi.org/10.5951/jresematheduc.45.1.0062).

Feedback should identify the error, the violated condition, and the next correction. “Wrong” and “great job” alone give little direction. [Wisniewski et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087). Explanations are useful when they connect reasons; demanding an explanation of every tiny step can add load. [Rittle-Johnson, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmathb.2024.101192). Concrete examples and formal statements should be explicitly connected. [IES practice guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/20072004.pdf).

AI can help during practice while leaving independent performance weaker if it supplies answers too freely. One high-school mathematics field experiment found this risk and evidence that tutoring safeguards mitigated it. That result motivates hidden solutions and independent retests here; it does not diagnose any particular student's result. [Bastani et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122).

## What demanding theory courses actually recommend

Stanford CS103 separates creative problem-solving from formal proof writing, explicitly teaches assumptions and conclusions, and checks variable scope and justified claims. Its exam review advises working on weak material and writing an answer before consulting solutions. We adopt those observable course practices. They are teaching guidance, not evidence that every high-scoring student follows one secret routine. [Guide to proofs](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs103/guide_to_proofs), [Proofwriting checklist](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs103/proofwriting_checklist), [Exam review, pages 3–6](https://web.stanford.edu/class/archive/cs/cs103/cs103.1238/lectures/21/Review%20Session%20Slides.pdf).

There is no established single “best” method for every learner and every task, and university prestige is not causal evidence. The strongest practical combination here is accurate modelling, meaningful independent attempts, specific feedback, delayed retrieval, and varied exam tasks. We adjust it from demonstrated work rather than claiming it already works for you.

## A practical study block

Use about 45–60 minutes as a starting structure: 5–10 minutes recalling an earlier task, 15–20 minutes following one worked example, 15–20 minutes attempting and checking a fresh problem, and a few minutes recording the next repair. These times are planning choices. If a concept breaks, spend the block repairing it; reading more pages doesn't compensate for a missing dependency.

For daily reading on a phone, study the explanation and trace a small example. For exam practice, use paper and close the lesson. During a mixed mock, set the timer, keep solutions shut, and review only after the block. Use the attempt record to note solution exposure. A problem whose answer you remember is useful recall practice, but is not a fresh independent test.

## What a 90+ target requires

Aim to complete representative unseen or meaningfully changed questions under the actual exam constraints, with correct constructions, complete proofs, and enough time to check the requested format. Review with the supplied rubric and official solutions, and ask for a mathematical review of your own written work when available. An answer that “looks familiar” is not evidence of that level. No reading-completion percentage or practice score guarantees 90+.

Before an exam: confirm current scope and logistics; account for every requested subpart; respect state and alphabet limits; test ε and shortest legal inputs; check all nondeterministic branches when a tree is required; finish TM simulations; name the contradiction or prove both directions as appropriate.
